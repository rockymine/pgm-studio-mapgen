The Freeform Leap

In three days one agent session built 37 PGM boards in Python without the studio's authoring pipeline, and distilled what they shared into a library, pgmvox. This report takes the branch apart: what was built, how a board is made, what the studio does that pgmvox does not, what pgmvox does that the studio cannot, and what of each a studio 2.0 has to keep.

Sources: the branch's code, plans and reports · three Sonnet helper reports (committed beside this one) · the author's playtest notes of 10 Oct · a controlled experiment, one brief built both ways

37boards on the branch: 20 bespoke, 17 on the library
13game modes, against the studio's 4
7,737lines of pgmvox (0.18), in 32 modules
9–29minutes per board once the library existed

The short answer

No single change made the leap. Seven things did, and they compound. The controlled experiment adds the eighth, which keeps the rest honest: with the model, brief, lessons and twenty minutes held equal, the studio and pgmvox made boards of comparable quality. The leap needed the open substrate and Opus's hours, your review rounds and bespoke code on top of it. The first is the one the others depend on: pgmvox has no vocabulary between the author and the block. The studio's document model is a closed set of words (rectangles on a plan grid, seven prop kinds, axis-aligned houses, a heightfield per layer), so anything outside that set cannot be built at all. In pgmvox a missing thing costs 5 to 60 lines of code. Every other cause is how the agent spent that freedom well.

  1. An open substrate.

    A World is two numpy arrays. A mill wheel, a broken bridge over the void, a cave behind a waterfall, a house at 38°, a karst tower of stacked rings or a ship's hull is just more blocks. Riftwater has 71 block ids and 50 structures of 100+ blocks; the studio's best board on a similar brief has 37 and 31.

  2. Places before relief.

    Your ruling “A board is a small world” (committed 21:34 on 7 Oct, one hour before Riftwater began) made every plan a list of places, each with a reason and a way in. The studio runs got the same ruling and improved, but could only build the part of their places the vocabulary had words for.

  3. The plan is code, checked and drawn.

    A per-block raster with storeys, every number printed beside its target, and one annotated sheet with true-scale sections. Lantern Karst went through five plan rounds in 43 minutes before a block was placed.

  4. The agent looks at each place up close.

    The studio already lets an agent see: isometrics, x-rays, sections, and eye views with the real block textures aimed at any named thing. What pgmvox adds is framing. Its isometric takes a box, so every place in the plan gets its own picture at four or five pixels a block; its sections follow any line; one run draws the whole set. A studio isometric is always the whole board, so a house is a few pixels.

  5. It accumulated.

    The 20 bespoke boards were 1,300–4,100 lines each; after an assessment measured 15,800 lines of copies, the library took them over, and the next boards were 350–1,600 lines and 9–29 minutes. Every report ends with a “what I wanted, how hard, what the studio would need” table, and the library grew from those rows.

  6. Style as a grammar.

    Brittlebush and Claywork are rules over sections and faces (courses read down from a rim, an accent bay laid whole or not at all, fills that decline), with the blocks in a separate Style. That is a layer above the studio's rim/wall/surface/fill buckets, not a replacement for them.

  7. Movement read as 1.8 plays it.

    A voxel walk that opens doors, climbs ladders, swims and jumps; a tick model for falls, fall damage, knockback and jump pads; sight lines. These made the arcade boards possible, and they caught faults before anything was built.

What it gave up is exactly what the studio stamps and gates: floating goal markers, build-zone outlines, the defence wall with its chests, the wool room and its loot, a kit paired to the goal's material, a map.xml codec and a 266-rule catalogue. Of the 30 items in your playtest notes, 9 are faults the studio would have prevented by itself, and most of the rest are faults that only exist because pgmvox can build stairs, ladders, joins and forests at all.

The experiment: one brief, built both ways

Two briefs, Slatefold (capture the wool: a slate hamlet on two terraced hillsides across the void) and Abbeymoor (destroy the monument: abbey hills, a village, a bog, a crypt with a way up near a monument), each built once through a local studio and once with pgmvox. All four builds were by Sonnet, from the same reading list, the same twelve playtest lessons and no author review. The briefs and the full comparison are in analysis/freeform-vs-studio/experiment/.

With the model, the brief, the lessons and about twenty minutes held equal, the two tools made boards of comparable quality. Neither pair shows the gap Riftwater against Russetford suggested, and each builder met almost every lesson. So the leap on the branch took the open substrate and what was spent on it: Opus, hours per board, rounds of your review, and bespoke code (Riftwater is 3,379 lines; these are 1,332 and 1,507). pgmvox raises the ceiling of what a board can be far more than it raises the floor of what a short run makes.

How a pgmvox map is made

The order is fixed in pgmvox/run.py, and the agent guide says where a board goes right or wrong: “every expensive failure here was a plan failure.”

plan.py + PLAN.md→plan check→sketch sheet→author review→gen.py→write (studio's Anvil writer)→map.xml→renders→read-back walk→REPORT.md

The plan states five things

An identity sentence; the arrangement; the numbers, each with a target (“lanes 12 to 16 wide”, “the walk from a goal to the enemy spawn three to four times the walk to its own”); the places, each with what it is, why a player goes there and how they get there; and the look, decided now (biome, three tone families, which material plays which role). The plan is a Raster holding every column's floor height and kind, with the symmetry applied inside the plan so the checker measures both halves, and with storey(n) rasters for caves, roofs and decks. The generator, the sketch, map.xml and the read-back all import the same plan.py, so they cannot disagree.

The sketch is reviewed before anything is built

One sheet: the board from above with floor heights written on every piece, places named, team markers mirrored; the routes arrowed with their jumps; true-scale sections across every height change and unrolled along the routes that matter; and the check table, every measurement against its target with misses in red. “A red row is resolved or explained before the build.”

The build adds only the look

“The generator reads the plan; it decides nothing the plan states.” Terrain, landforms, routes, buildings, facades and solids are free to use or to write fresh, because a board that wants a shape the library lacks writes it locally, and the second board to need it moves it into the library with a test. The read-back then walks the built blocks from each spawn (doors, ladders, swimming, jumps), asks every objective to check itself, looks for footing that would fall and water standing against air, and draws the plan's names over the built world.

Why this beats a zone plan

The studio's plan is rectangles on a 5-block grid, fanned through the symmetry, with no stack, no paint and no dressing; its walk is eight-connected places with a scramble and a free drop, and no ladders, jumps or pads. The freeform plan is per block and per storey, carries a capture-aware walk that prices bridging, and prints arrivals per team, jump gaps and sight lines. A plan that can state a cave can be reviewed for its cave.

The terrain, walked backwards

The library's terrain example, the Vale, is fifteen operations on one heightfield. Replayed one at a time, each step's output kept, it shows how pgmvox thinks about ground: start from noise, then apply named landforms in an order the board chooses. Each landform only cuts or only lifts, so they compose without one undoing another. The Recipe Inspector lets you step through these with what each one changed highlighted, cut sections anywhere, and play with the noise.

The noise is the least of it

noise.fbm(cell, octaves, gain) is a random lattice every cell blocks, smoothly interpolated, plus the same at half the cell and half the strength, octave by octave. ridged is one minus its absolute value, which puts crests where the noise crosses zero. That is the whole of the randomness, and it only makes the canvas. Everything a player reads as a place comes after it, from operations that each know what they are making:

  • watercourse samples the ground along a path and takes the running minimum, so the bed never climbs. It holds the water level in reaches and steps down in falls only where the ground drops two or more and a reach has run twelve blocks, then raises every bank cell to the water so no water stands against air.
  • canyon cuts a floor of a fraction of the width, with walls rising as distance to a power (3 is near-cliff), cut in ledges, with a floor that may never climb along the path.
  • butte and spire are one family: a cliffed stack with a talus apron, blunt or jagged by taper and jag.
  • route.find searches 48 headings over the ground with costs for grade, turning, climbing and bridging. The turn cost is what makes a path switch back: without it, 71 zig-zags; with it, 5 legs.

Then the paint reads the angle

terrain.lay reads every column's slope with the studio's own gradient (a test holds them equal on 21,972 cells). It chooses the top block by angle (grass to 30°, coarse dirt to 42°, andesite to 55°, stone past it), puts soil three deep on gentle ground and none on a cliff, and fills the rock with Strata: weighted beds of stone, andesite, clay and terracotta that dip and fold under a noise offset. A cliff is rock to its face, and its beds show.

Marks against landforms

The studio's relief works the other way round. A mark is a constraint (this disc is at 40; this line runs from 40 down to 30), and a relaxation solves the smoothest surface that honours every mark at once. Its extremes can only sit where a mark put one, so nothing appears that nobody asked for. That is a real virtue, and it is also why marks feel hard to control: what one mark does depends on the reach, the rim and every other mark, and it shows only after the whole field is solved. Below is the studio's own ReliefSolver, called directly.

The two are not rivals. The studio's pushes are already operations applied after the solve, so pgmvox's landforms are more operations of that kind. A board can start with marks where the author wants exact heights, then add a canyon, a river that knows it is water, and beds that show on the cliffs. The solver stays; the vocabulary after it grows.

Why the noise rolls

You asked why the studio's noise feels grainy the moment it is turned up, while pgmvox's sections roll and leave flat ground for houses and paths. Both are the same kind of noise: random values on a lattice, interpolated between, summed in octaves that halve in size and strength each time. pgmvox interpolates with a cubic spline; the studio's PatternNoise blends neighbours with a smoothstep. To find what actually differs, I ported the studio's noise exactly and ran each with the other's settings.

The settings, not the algorithm, make the grain. The studio's noise at pgmvox's settings (panel 3) is as walkable as pgmvox's own (80% flat steps against 81%, 56% of the ground in large flat patches against 60%). pgmvox's noise at the studio's grain settings (panel 4) is as grainy as the studio's. Two numbers decide it:

  • The finest octave. The relief grain's default scale is 9 with three octaves, and integer halving makes the cells 9, 4 and 2 blocks. The last octave changes every two blocks, which is the size of a block step, so after rounding it scatters one-block steps everywhere. The Vale's finest octave is 6 blocks across at a fifteenth of the weight.
  • Amplitude over feature size. A slope is roughly π × amplitude ÷ feature size. The Vale is ±14 over 48 (a ratio of 0.29). Grain 4 at scale 9 is 0.44; grain 14 at scale 9 is 1.56, and then 23% of all neighbouring steps are two blocks or more: a wall every few blocks. Keeping the ratio under about 0.3, and the finest octave above about five blocks, is what keeps ground walkable.

The interpolation does matter a little. A smoothstep has zero slope at every lattice point, so at large scales the contours run straight along the grid; the studio panel at scale 48 shows square-cornered terraces where the pgmvox panel is round. A cubic spline, or gradient noise such as Perlin or simplex, removes it.

And noise is not where the flat places come from. pgmvox uses noise only as the canvas. The flat ground a house stands on comes from operations that flatten: a terrace step, a butte's top, a road graded into the hill, hold at a bank, and a house's own site eased into the slope. A recipe is a chain of such operations over noise. Game terrain is usually built this way: Minecraft's own world generation, since 1.18, is a graph of noise fields and operators written as JSON ("density functions"), and tools such as World Machine and Gaea are node editors over the same idea. The Hytale editor you mentioned is the same family. A linear recipe is the simplest such graph, and nodes can come later if a board ever needs one operation's output in two places.

Strata that follow the land

Here the studio is ahead. Its height band stack can follow the ground (TP26): the bands' datum rises by a share of the surface averaged over reach cells either side, so the beds carry the land's broad tilt while a hill cuts through them. pgmvox has two settings and neither is that. Its beds are level with a dip and a fold, which ignores the land; or their offset is the surface itself, which wraps every bump in a thin rind.

Bringing it over is one line in pgmvox: the bed offset becomes follow × (the ground averaged over ±reach), which is exactly the studio's definition. It is a good example of the merge running both ways.

One renderer

You're right that seeing the board is not new. The grammar, step by step

A grammar board is a list of sections: boxes at one height. The grammar reads every column's depth in from its section's edge, then visits each section once, in a fixed order: body under every column; a face where the ground beside falls two or more, its courses read down from the rim; an accent bay every so many along a face, laid whole or not at all; a seam where two sections meet at one height; a fill over the interior, chosen by the section's shape; motifs on top. The blocks all live in a Style, so the same plan can be built in Brittlebush or in Claywork. Below, each picture adds one rule to the one before it.

This is the plan road you found with Brittlebush III. The studio's planner already draws everything the grammar reads (pieces, heights, roles, zones); a brittle cell is five blocks, which is the studio's grid. What it cannot say yet is held in board tables: which cells are hollow, which deck is raised, where a rectangle splits into two sections, which fill a section must take, where the arrows go. Those are a handful of fields on a piece, and with them a bound style turns a drawn plan into a finished board in one step.

Why the houses look good

The difference is where the taste lives. A studio house style is 77 to 258 values (a median of 146): every band of every storey's wall, the posts, beams, gable windows, doorway head, plate and surface are data, and any combination of them is legal. An agent filling those in has to rediscover good proportions every time, and the bad combinations look bad. A pgmvox style is ten material slots: ground wall, upper wall, frame wood, gable, floor, roof, stair, slab, door, window. The articulation is code and always runs: a post at every corner and every four blocks along an upper storey, a laid log at each storey's head turned along its wall, jettied joists, the gable filled to the roof's underside, the attic cleared, a chimney from the floor to two above the crown with a cap. Any choice of materials gets a well-built house.

Three other things help. The studio's doorway is a closed set (air, cobweb, glass) because one rule serves wool rooms and houses alike, so a house can never have a real door; pgmvox's houses do. Frame puts a house at any heading with the studio's own six roof forms. And there is no footing: pgmvox fills every column under the plate down to the ground and eases the ground round the footprint over two blocks, so a house sits on a slope as if it were built there. The studio's footing was a one-block ring around the plate, now off by default and complained of (HS7).

The cost is in the other direction. pgmvox houses are one rectangle a storey, with no wings or porches, and they have no window/door rule (49% of sampled houses have a pane beside the door). The studio has both. The meeting point is an archetype as a library row: the framing rules of a kind of house, with the studio's styles mapped onto archetypes as their materials.

Placement and markers do different jobs, and a goal wants both. A marker says where to go from across the map; it does not open a line of sight. The studio's Slatefold wool rooms carry a cube 25 blocks over each one and are still seen from 4% and 18% of the ground near them, because the wool is inside a room by design. A destroy goal wants the opposite: placement that keeps it in view on the last stretch of the walk, and a marker for the first. Two gates would hold both: a goal seen from at least a quarter of the attackers' ground within 60 blocks, and in sight for the last 40 blocks of their walk. Both thresholds are proposals and should be calibrated against the corpus before they become rules.

The stamps you traded away

The bedrock wall with its defence chests, the defence chest at every goal, the wool room's gear, the floating markers and the outline are all one subsystem in the studio (StructureStamper, DefenseChest, GoalMarkerStamper, BuildMarkerStamper, the wool-room stamp), and none of it runs on a freeform world. pgmvox has been copying them one at a time (wool_chests on 10 Oct at 00:30, defence_chests at 00:46). Copying is the slow road: these are deterministic stamps over objectives the freeform plan already states as objects, so they could run over a pgmvox world unchanged.

Build-region fidelity

Two different things got looser. The zone itself is exact (a per-column mask equal to the plan's), but it is written only as block 36 plus a void filter and maxbuildheight, so nothing outside the board's own read-back checks that the mask matches the plan. And the studio's richer build intent (rectangle unions with holes, over-void areas, the outline, the EZ checks) has no counterpart.

Angled houses and the tower wool

Houses at any angle come from build.Frame: every block asks where its centre falls in the building's own axes, walls are closed against eight neighbours so a 45° wall has no gaps, and the roof is the studio's RoofField ported and tested cell for cell against 300 studio roofs, laid in cubes and slabs where stairs cannot face the slope. Hollowcrown uses sixteen headings on one half. Hoarfrost Reach's lighthouse is board-local: a striped 9×9 shaft, slits, a ladder with a hatch every five courses and the wool room at y 94 under a glass lantern.

Studio 2.0

The goal is one tool, and the hard part is the one you named: the studio lets a person see what an agent did at every level, change it and diff it, while a pgmvox board is a program whose middle is invisible. Everything that made the boards good (landforms in a chosen order, the grammar, angled frames, props with a reason) lives in exactly that invisible middle. So the question is how to make the middle a document without losing what it can say. The full argument is analysis/freeform-vs-studio/STUDIO-2-DESIGN.md; the Recipe Inspector shows what a person would see.

D is the destination and A is the first step. A recipe is an ordered list of operations, each with an id and its parameters, run deterministically over a shared state (the heightfield, named masks like sea and roads, the block volume), with every step's output kept. A made thing is the escape hatch: a block volume the agent generated locally, uploaded with its source for provenance, placed and turned as an object. A made thing the second board needs becomes a library operation, which is the rule pgmvox already lives by.

A seventy-line interpreter maps each operation name onto one pgmvox call. A second version of the recipe moved the butte and widened the canyon: two steps' parameters changed, and six steps' effect changed, because the roads re-routed over 3,261 columns nobody touched. A diff has to show both layers, and the second is the one to warn about. For scale, Russetford's stored layout is 378 KB and its refinement 357 KB, generated by a 426-line script: a recipe states a landform where a layout states its result.

How the pieces fit

  • The studio's relief solver is one operation in the recipe (field.solve), and pgmvox's landforms come after it, as its pushes do now.
  • The planner stays the front door. A drawn plan compiles into the recipe's first steps, and with a grammar style bound, its pieces become sections in one step (grammar.from_plan, which is studioplan today).
  • Upload and configure survives untouched. An imported world is a recipe of one step, a made thing covering the board, and Configure works over it as it does now.
  • Styles stay library rows. Grammar styles and house archetypes are data like themes; the rules that make them look right (a bay laid whole or not at all, the framing of a house) stay code.
  • The stamps and gates run last, over everything. Markers, outlines, wool rooms, defence walls, kit pairing and the export gate stop depending on how the ground was made.

The model's loop is ORDER-OF-WORK's: places on the outline with numbers and targets; terrain steps, read slopes and walk, adjust; carves, read the x-ray; made ground; the intent, then stamps and gates. A person opens the inspector, scrubs the steps, changes a parameter or leaves a note on a step, and the next agent run receives each hand edit as an SR1 naming the step and the parameter, as it does today.

The order of work

  1. Stamps over a made world, now. Import a pgmvox world with its intent and run WorldBuilder's stamps and the export gate over it. Fix pgmvox's two house defects. Add the playtest's gates to its read-back: a goal visible from where attackers arrive, stairs attached, no ladder in water, joins that open, a tree-cover ceiling per mode.
  2. The recipe as a stored document, with made things and step reads, holding steps pgmvox computed. The studio shows and diffs a recipe before it can run one.
  3. The operations ported into the studio, family by family, in the order the boards used them: field and landform, paint and beds, carves (TS140), houses with a heading, the grammar, routes.
  4. One plan, with polygons, storeys and the grammar's per-piece fields, whose numbers-with-targets become rules.

A second analysis, cross-checked

The freeform branch's own session had a Sonnet agent write freeform/QUALITY-LEAP.md on the same question. It lists the same mechanisms (per-block control, paint by slope and tilted beds, undersides, angled frames with the studio's roofs, the grammar, props, the plan-sketch-walk loop) and ranks the floating objective marker first among what to adopt. Three things it states more sharply than this report: the studio's painter "rewrites the surface, no new geometry, only materials", so every studio look is a finish over a shape already decided; WX12 holds a room inside its plan piece, which is part of why the studio's wool rooms sat where the plan's rectangles were; and a freeform board enforces only the rules its checker remembers to re-measure.

Two of its claims do not hold. It asks for a slope axis in the studio's themes, which exists (TP24), and the studio's strata are ahead (TP26). And it says gameplay "barely changed": the XML is valid, but nine of your thirty playtest faults are ones the studio catches, and the experiment's pgmvox Abbeymoor added a tenth, a 27-block obsidian cube where DC3 allows three. Its "nothing here is controlled" was true when written; the experiment now moves weight off the tool and onto what was spent on it. The full cross-check is analysis/freeform-vs-studio/QUALITY-LEAP-CROSSCHECK.md.

Method and limits

  • Sources. Three Sonnet helper reports (pgmvox anatomy and census; studio inventory; same-brief comparison), each checked against the code before use and committed under analysis/freeform-vs-studio/agent-reports/. I viewed the renders of all 42 board render sets myself.
  • Not played. Nothing on the new library boards has been opened in PGM. The playtest rounds on older boards found over ten faults the read-back could not see.
  • Plan and built walks disagree. pgmvox's plan walk is octile and its built walk four-way; goal ratios drift 15–40% between them, and every trial board hit it.
  • Rendering is a confound. The studio's textured pictures and pgmvox's flat ones are not comparable; where the report compares looks, both are drawn in pgmvox's renderer.
  • The brief is a confound. Riftwater was not built to the studio boards' brief, and it had a ruling they did not. The experiment holds brief, ruling, lessons and model fixed.
  • Counts are heuristics. “Built” counts treat cobblestone as built; tree counts are approximate; enclosed-air classes are geometric guesses.
