PUT …/sketch — three house styles, each one field off the `gable` panel's base. A style is checked
at the store door, and the message names the JSON path.

### an `arched` window whose block is a glass pane
  PUT 400
  HS1 refusal — windows.block (102) is not a stair. The form turns its corners by a stair's own facing;
  anything else builds without the diamond or the rounded corners the form is named for.

### beams of one wood over posts of another
  PUT 400
  HS4 refusal — beams are oak and post is spruce. A post, the beam ends docking against it and the course
  they are the ends of are one frame, so they are cut from one wood.
  HS4 refusal — beams are oak and storeys[0].post is spruce. A post, the beam ends docking against it and
  the course they are the ends of are one frame, so they are cut from one wood.
  HS4 refusal — beams are oak and storeys[1].post is spruce. A post, the beam ends docking against it and
  the course they are the ends of are one frame, so they are cut from one wood.
  HS4 refusal — beams are oak and the wall's laid log is spruce. A post, the beam ends docking against it
  and the course they are the ends of are one frame, so they are cut from one wood.

### beams with no laid-log course anywhere in the style
  PUT 400
  HS9 refusal — the building lays beams of spruce and no course of any of its walls is a laid log, so the
  ends run out of masonry with no timber behind them. Lay the storey's top course in a laidLog of the same
  material, or drop the beams.

The card's own layout is stored again after these.

**A window's form and its block are one decision.** `arched` and `stairLattice` turn their corners
by a stair's own facing, so the block has to be a stair; the `windows-arched` panel states both.

**A frame is one wood.** Beams and posts are the same timber or the style is refused, which is a
rule about the building and a confusing way to learn that `162` is acacia and dark oak is `162:1`.

**Beams want something to be beam ends of.** A style with `beams.any` and no laid-log course
anywhere in it — its roof's verge, its walls, its storeys — has log ends running out of nothing.
